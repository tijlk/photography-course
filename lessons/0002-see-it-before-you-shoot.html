Photography course · Lesson 0002

See it before you shoot

Mirrorless viewfinders and in-body stabilisation, the two changes that fix what the self-check caught.

About 15 minutes Phase 2 of 5 · Camera tech since 2013 Builds on lesson 0001

Your self-check misses had a theme: the camera's automatic guesses (metering, white balance) and the rules of thumb for correcting them. On a 30D you found those mistakes on the rear screen, after the shot. Two technologies that became standard after you stopped change that. This lesson covers both, and the next one covers autofocus and high ISO.

1 · The viewfinder now shows the photo

A DSLR shows you the scene through a mirror. In a mirrorless camera there's no mirror: the lens always shines light onto the sensor, and the camera shows the sensor's picture on a screen.1 Put that screen behind an eyepiece and you have an electronic viewfinder (EVF), which can show a preview of how the photo will actually look, something an optical finder can't do.2

Two settings matter most for you:

  • Exposure simulation. With it on, the viewfinder brightness is close to the brightness of the final photo, and it changes when you set exposure compensation.3 In auto or semi-auto modes, that shows you whether the meter has been fooled.4
  • White balance preview. The viewfinder shows the effect of the current white balance setting, whether it's auto, a preset or custom.4

The trade-offs: an EVF can lag a little, especially with long exposures, and its screen has less dynamic range than your eye looking through an optical finder.2

Try it: the snowfield

A bright snowfield, metered automatically. First tap Optical finder (30D) and move the slider: nothing changes. Then switch back and fix the photo before taking it. Aim for white snow, with the histogram not piled against the right edge.

Try it: the cabin

Evening inside a cabin lit by old-style incandescent bulbs. The camera's white balance is set for daylight. Make the grey card neutral.

These are simplified simulations for building intuition, not colour-accurate renderings of any camera.

2 · Stabilisation moved into the body

Your 17-55 has stabilisation in the lens. In-body image stabilisation (IBIS) instead moves the sensor itself to counter camera shake.5 A body can stabilise lenses that have none of their own, including older lenses and other makers' lenses. Some bodies need you to enter the focal length for a lens that can't report it, which doesn't work well for zooms.5

How to use the number on the spec sheet:

  1. Start from the handheld rule, using the 35mm-equivalent focal length: slowest speed ≈ 1/(equivalent focal length).5 This is exactly the crop-factor step from lesson 0001.
  2. Each stop of stabilisation doubles the time you can hold. Stabilisation typically allows 2 to 5.5 stops slower.5 Manufacturers measure this under a standard test from CIPA, the camera industry body (DC-011, revised in 2024), so treat the figure as a lab result, not a promise.6
The catch

Stabilisation only reduces blur from small movements of your hands. It does nothing about a moving subject.5 It helps landscapes at dusk, but a walking elk or a running child still needs a fast shutter speed.

What this means for buying

When you compare cameras in phase 3, check two things: whether the camera has IBIS and how many stops it's rated (and whether that figure is for the body alone or for a particular lens), and whether the EVF offers exposure simulation. Most mirrorless bodies do, but check the manual. Both are now on the Modern camera tech reference sheet.

Primary source

Fujifilm: What You See Is What You Get! is a short manufacturer guide to everything a mirrorless viewfinder can preview, including exposure, white balance and depth of field, and when to turn the preview off.
