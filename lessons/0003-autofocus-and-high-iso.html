Autofocus and high ISO

A camera that finds the eye, and what an ISO spec does and doesn't tell you.

About 12 minutes Phase 2 of 5 · Camera tech since 2013 Builds on lesson 0002

This is the last "what changed" lesson before we choose a camera. It covers the two changes that matter most for wildlife at dusk, family on the move and stars after dark.

1 · Autofocus that finds the subject

Your 30D had 9 AF points, and you chose between One-Shot and AI Servo.1 You picked a point and kept it on the subject yourself.

A modern mirrorless camera can find the subject for you. Using the Canon EOS R6 manual as an example:

  • Face + Tracking: the camera detects the faces of people or animals and tracks them. If no face is found, it chooses a focus point across the whole frame by itself.2
  • Subject to detect: choose People, Animals or Vehicles. On the R6, "animals" means dogs, cats and birds.2 Other animals aren't promised, so an elk may or may not be recognised. Check this for any camera you consider.
  • Eye detection: focuses on the eye of a person or animal. The manual warns that eyes may not always be detected, depending on the subject and conditions.2
  • Servo AF keeps focusing while you half-press, for moving subjects. One-Shot AF focuses once and locks, for still subjects.3 These are the same ideas as the 30D's AI Servo and One-Shot.
Where autofocus still struggles

The manual lists low light, low contrast (such as plain blue sky), and night scenes or points of light as hard conditions.2 For stars, plan to focus manually, using the magnified view.

2 · High ISO: read the spec carefully

On paper the change is huge. The 30D went up to ISO 3200.1 The R6's normal range is ISO 100–102400, expandable to the equivalent of 204800. Canon warns that the expanded setting adds noise and loses resolution.4

But the top of the range says little about how clean ISO 3200 or 6400 looks. The biggest factor in noise is how much light the whole sensor collects. At the same f-number, a larger sensor collects more light, so it shows less noise.5

Do the maths once

A full-frame sensor has the 1.6× crop factor squared, about 2.6×, the area of APS-C.6 2.6× the light is about 1⅓ stops, so at the same settings full frame shows roughly the noise of an APS-C body set 1⅓ stops lower. That's one real input to the full frame vs APS-C question in phase 3. (Your 17-55 is an EF-S lens made for APS-C, which is part of that question too.)

For the night sky, National Park Service rangers suggest the widest aperture your lens has, ISO starting around 400 and raised as needed, and 10–20 second exposures.7 Another NPS guide suggests starting near 14 seconds and adjusting from your test shots.8 Both say results depend on your camera and lens, so how good a camera is at high ISO decides how far you can push. Night skies get their own lesson later.
