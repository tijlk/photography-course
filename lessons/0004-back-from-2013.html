Photography course · Lesson 0004

Back from 2013

You fell asleep holding a 30D and woke up in 2026. Here's what changed, and what to check when buying.

About 15 minutes Phase 2 of 5 · Camera tech since 2013 (overview) Ties together lessons 0002 and 0003

Lessons 0002 and 0003 went deep on four features. This lesson is the overview. Each change gets three lines: what changed, what it means for you, and what to check when you buy. Everything worth checking is collected in the checklist at the end.

Examples come from Canon's EOS R6 manual because it's thorough and online. Other brands have equivalents under different names.

The big shift

DSLRs are over

Changed
Mirrorless cameras began outselling DSLRs in 2021. As of 2025, every major brand except Pentax has stopped making new consumer DSLR models.1
For you
Buy mirrorless. A used DSLR is cheap and still works, but new bodies and lenses are all being made for mirrorless mounts.
Check
That you're buying into a system that's still getting new lenses.

New lens mounts, and adapters for old lenses

Changed
Removing the mirror let makers move the lens closer to the sensor, so each brand launched a new mount. Canon's RF mount (2018) has a 20 mm flange distance, against 44 mm for EF and EF-S. Canon makes adapters that let EF and EF-S lenses fit RF bodies.2
For you
Your 17-55 can go on a Canon RF body with an adapter. On an APS-C RF body it behaves as on your 30D (about 27–88mm equivalent). On a full-frame RF body the camera crops to APS-C, so you'd only use part of the sensor you paid for.2
Check
Mount and sensor size together decide whether your old lens still makes sense.

The lens ecosystem matters more than the body

Changed
Mounts now differ in which third-party lenses they allow. On Canon RF, third-party makers currently offer manual-focus lenses and autofocus lenses that cover only APS-C. Several third-party full-frame autofocus lenses were discontinued at Canon's request.2
For you
You'll probably keep a mount for years and replace the body sooner. Lens choice and price can decide the system.
Check
Which lenses you'd want for the trip (wide, standard, tele) exist for that mount, and from whom.

Seeing and focusing

The viewfinder is a screen

Changed
The EVF can preview exposure, white balance and depth of field, and overlay focus peaking, zebras (highlight warnings) and face or eye tracking.3 Covered in lesson 0002.
For you
Fewer surprises on the rear screen afterwards. Turn exposure simulation on.
Check
Try the viewfinder in person if you can. Look for exposure simulation in the manual.

Autofocus moved onto the sensor

Changed
On-sensor autofocus doesn't need the calibration a DSLR's separate AF sensor did (the old front- and back-focus fiddling). Newer AI autofocus has been shown to beat DSLRs on speed and accuracy.3 Subject and eye detection are covered in lesson 0003.
For you
Sharper results with fast lenses, and wildlife or kids tracked across the frame.
Check
Which subjects it detects. Does "animals" go beyond pets and birds?

Touchscreens

Changed
Touch to pick the focus point, or touch to take the picture. Some cameras let you drag the focus point on the screen while your eye is at the viewfinder.4
For you
Moving the focus point is fast, which helps with off-centre subjects in landscapes.
Check
Personal preference. Try handling it if you can.

Taking the picture

Silent electronic shutter, with a catch

Changed
Many cameras can shoot with no shutter sound, and burst rates went up. On the R6 the electronic shutter shoots up to about 20 frames per second. The catches: fast-moving subjects may look distorted, and speeds slower than 0.5 s aren't available.5 The distortion happens because the sensor is read line by line rather than all at once (a "rolling shutter").6
For you
Great for a sleeping child or skittish wildlife. Switch to the mechanical shutter for fast action and for star shots longer than 0.5 s.
Check
Whether it has a mechanical shutter too (most do), and the electronic shutter's limits.

Stabilisation in the body

Changed
The sensor moves to cancel hand shake, so it works with any lens. Covered in lesson 0002.
For you
Dusk landscapes handheld. It does nothing for moving subjects.
Check
Whether it has IBIS, and its CIPA rating.

The camera corrects the lens

Changed
Using profiles of each lens, the camera corrects vignetting (dark corners), distortion, chromatic aberration (coloured fringes) and diffraction.7
For you
Cleaner JPEGs straight out of the camera. Lightroom applies the same kind of corrections to RAW files.
Check
Nothing to compare. Just know it's there, and that adapted or third-party lenses may lack correction data.

Built-in tricks you used to need gear for

Changed
Built-in interval timer, so no external remote is needed for star sequences.8 Focus bracketing: the camera shifts focus between shots so software can merge them into one image sharp from front to back.9
For you
Wildflowers sharp in front of a sharp mountain. Star sequences without extra kit.
Check
Nice extras, not deal-breakers.

Image quality

Sensor area and high ISO

Changed
Usable ISO went way up, but noise is still set mainly by how much light the whole sensor collects. Covered in lesson 0003.
For you
Full frame vs APS-C is about 1⅓ stops of noise. That matters for stars and dusk wildlife.
Check
Test images at ISO 3200–6400, not the top ISO figure.

Power, cards and your phone

Batteries run down sooner

Changed
Mirrorless cameras power the sensor and viewfinder all the time, so batteries last less long than in DSLRs.3 Many can charge over USB in the camera. The R6, for example, does it with Canon's USB power adapter and only with certain battery models.10
For you
On a camper trip, plan for spare batteries and a way to charge from the vehicle.
Check
The shots-per-charge rating, whether it charges over USB in the camera (and with which chargers), and the price of a spare battery.

Your memory cards won't fit

Changed
The 30D used CompactFlash.11 Current cameras use SD cards (the R6 takes SD UHS-I and UHS-II)12 or CFexpress, a newer standard with three sizes (Type A, B and C).13
For you
Budget for new cards. Your plan of several cards, never wiped, still works.
Check
Which card type and speed class the camera needs, and what those cards cost.

Two card slots: a backup with no effort

Changed
Some bodies have two slots and can write every photo to both cards (Canon calls it "Rec. to multiple").14
For you
This fits your trip rule of no backups on the road. The camera makes the backup as you shoot. Keep the two cards in different places.
Check
Dual slots with a mirror or duplicate mode. High on your list.

The camera talks to your phone

Changed
Over Bluetooth and Wi-Fi, a phone app can browse and save the camera's photos, receive photos sent from the camera, control it remotely, and add GPS locations from the phone.15
For you
That's how you'll send a few JPEGs to the family chat on the road (a later lesson). Remote control also helps with tripod and star shots.
Check
That the brand's app is well reviewed for your phone.

What you can ignore

Much of a 2026 spec sheet is about video: resolutions, frame rates, recording limits. You've ruled out video, so don't pay extra for it.

The checklist

In rough order of importance for this trip:

  1. System: a mirrorless mount with the lenses you'll want (wide, standard, tele) at prices you accept.
  2. Sensor size vs your 17-55: APS-C keeps the lens fully useful. Full frame means the lens crops, and you'll probably buy new lenses.
  3. Two card slots with a "record to both" mode.
  4. Subject detection that covers the wildlife you expect, plus eye detection.
  5. IBIS and its CIPA rating.
  6. High-ISO test images at ISO 3200–6400.
  7. Battery: shots per charge, USB charging, and the cost of a spare.
  8. Card type and cost (SD or CFexpress).
  9. EVF with exposure simulation, and a phone app that works.
