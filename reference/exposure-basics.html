Exposure basics

The fundamentals on one printable page.

Stops

A stop halves or doubles the light. Every exposure setting moves in stops, so you can trade one setting for another stop for stop.2

Distance and focal length act as a ratio, so they matter most at close range.5 Hyperfocal distance is the focus distance that gives the most depth of field: everything from about half that distance to infinity looks acceptably sharp.5

Handheld shutter speed

Rule of thumb: the slowest safe handheld speed is about 1/(focal length) on 35mm film.4 On a crop sensor, use the equivalent focal length (focal length × crop factor).6 Stabilisation and good bracing allow slower speeds.

Your lens

The EF-S 17–55mm on a 1.6× APS-C body covers the same view as 27–88mm on full frame.7 Its effective focal length depends on the camera you buy, which is phase 3 of the course.

Metering & histogram

  • Meters assume the scene averages to middle grey (about 18% reflectance).8 Bright scenes come out too dark: use + compensation. Dark scenes come out too bright: use − compensation.9
  • On a histogram, the left is shadows, the middle is midtones and the right is highlights.10 Data piled against an edge means detail lost to pure black or pure white.
  • Sunny 16: in direct sun, f/16 at 1/ISO. For example, ISO 100 gives 1/100 s, or 1/125 s on cameras without 1/100.13

Lower kelvin looks warmer (more orange), higher kelvin looks cooler (more blue). Values from Wikipedia's table of light sources.11

RAW vs JPEG

A RAW file stores the camera's settings without applying them, so white balance, contrast and the rest can be redone in editing. It holds 12 or 14 bits per channel, against 8 for a JPEG.12 The costs are bigger files and needing to edit.
